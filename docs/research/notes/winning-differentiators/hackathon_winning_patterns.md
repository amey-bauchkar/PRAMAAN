# What wins national-level EdTech / placement / civic-tech hackathon screening rounds and finals in India

Scope note: research done 2026-11-18. Every item in "Cited Findings" is tied to a URL. Anything in "Inferences" is my reasoning, not a source claim. Source quality is flagged inline (OFFICIAL = organiser/rule-book; THIRD-PARTY = blog/vendor/aggregator, uncited weights; FIRST-PERSON = a team or judge's own account, unverifiable). Product novelty is out of scope (see docs/research/campus-placement-novelty-landscape.md).

## 1. QUASAR 5.0: what public information exists

### Takeaway
There is a public QUASAR hackathon series (flagship 24-hour national hackathon of Vasantdada Patil Pratishthan's College of Engineering & Visual Arts, VPPCOE, Sion, Mumbai) with editions 1.0 (2023), 2.0 (2024), 3.0 (March 2025) and 4.0 (24-25 Jan 2026). ### Cited Findings

### Inferences

- Because 3.0 had only 56 on-site teams and 2.0/1.0 prize pools were Rs 60-80k, the "~500 submissions" screening is plausibly a ~10:1 cut. That ratio is my inference from 56 teams in 3.0 and the user's stated ~500; it is not a published figure.
- The stage where judges "suggest implementation changes" for the 24-hour build rewards a team whose prototype is modular and who can show a visible before/after change after judge feedback (inference).
- Final-round framing is "business idea", so cost, sustainability and go-to-market slides likely matter more here than in pure technical hackathons (inference from the Phase 4 wording).

### Gaps

## 2. Official rubrics and weights of comparable events

### Takeaway
Only two sources publish real point weights: Google Solution Challenge (50 points: Impact 25 + Technology 25, each of 10 questions worth 5) and one Google-India "Prompt Wars" Solution Challenge 2026 page (Technical Merit 40, Innovation 25, Alignment With Cause 25, UX 10 in Phase 1). SIH publishes criteria WITHOUT weights. Everything else commonly quoted ("innovation 20-25%") comes from uncited third-party blogs. A consistent theme across all official rubrics: a working product, evidence from real users, and scalability are scored explicitly, while pure "novelty" is just one item among many.

### Cited Findings
**Smart India Hackathon (OFFICIAL, no weights)**
- SIH 2024 guidelines: ideas are evaluated by experts on "novelty of the idea, complexity, clarity and details in the prescribed format, feasibility, practicability, sustainability, scale of impact, user experience and potential for future work progression." — [SIH 2024 College SPOC Guidelines](https://sih.gov.in/letters/Guidelines-College-SPOC.pdf)
- Funnel facts from the same guidelines: only 500 ideas are accepted per problem statement (counter freezes at 500); the idea submission is a PDF presentation plus title and description; only 4-5 teams per problem statement may be selected for the finale; the sponsoring organisation is "not obligated to declare a winner"; prize Rs 1,00,000 per problem statement; ideas "must be new and must not have been present in any previous event/program of any sort." — [SIH 2024 College SPOC Guidelines](https://sih.gov.in/letters/Guidelines-College-SPOC.pdf)
- SIH 2025 scale: 68,766 teams submitted 72,165 ideas against 271 problem statements; 8,160 finalists from 727 institutes; finale at 60 nodal centres. — [Ministry of Education/PIB release (search summary; direct page returned 403)](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2201244)
- Third-party SIH guidance (unverified against official docs): national shortlisting looks at problem understanding, innovation, feasibility, completeness of submission; incomplete documentation can eliminate a strong idea. — [Where U Elevate blog](https://whereuelevate.com/blogs/smart-india-hackathon-2026)

**Google Solution Challenge (OFFICIAL structure, 2024 participant guide via SlideShare mirror)**
- 50 points = Impact 25 + Technology 25; each of 5 sub-items is 5 points.
 - Impact: clear challenge; SDG choice and reasoning; user feedback and iteration (three real user feedback points and what changed); success and results (metrics, goals or cause-and-effect); next steps for a larger audience.
 - Technology: architecture and why each product was chosen; completeness; code testing and iteration (one real coding challenge and how it was resolved, run instructions); demo video showing an "actual working application (not a mockup)"; scalability.
 — [2024 Solution Challenge Participant Guide (SlideShare)](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/2024-solution-challenge-participant-guidepptx/265566208)
- Demo video max 2 minutes; problem statement is one paragraph covering who is affected and how many, what, where/when, why it matters. — same guide
- The official FAQ confirms the 1-50 scale across Impact and Technology (reported via search summary of the FAQ page). — [Google Developers FAQ](https://developers.google.com/community/gdsc-solution-challenge/faq)
- 2024: 10 teams selected for live Demo Day; 3 winners (one Indian: Spoon Share, Deogiri Institute, Aurangabad: a food-donation map app) plus a People's Choice (Saheli, NIT Jalandhar: women's safety app). Google's announcement reports no usage numbers or pilots for any winner. — [Google blog](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/meet-the-students-that-are-changing-the-world-through-technology/)

**Solution Challenge 2026 India page (hosting organiser unclear: promptwars.in; treat as semi-official)**
- Phase 1 weights: Technical Merit 40%, Innovation and Creativity 25%, Alignment With Cause 25%, User Experience 10%. Phase 2 (top 106 teams): scalability, user impact, execution clarity, solution maturity/production readiness (no weights). Phase 3 (top 14, virtual finale, video up to 3 minutes): innovation, impact, technical depth, scalability, presentation quality. Phase 1 requires a live MVP link, deck, public GitHub repo and demo video. — [promptwars.in Solution Challenge 2026](https://promptwars.in/solutionchallenge2026.html)

**Microsoft Imagine Cup (OFFICIAL FAQ; no numeric weights)**
- Staged criteria: application review (startup profile, product/technical approach, problem and market opportunity, team execution potential); expert review (business readiness, technical excellence, customer value); final judging (enterprise customer validation, investability, technical excellence, customer value). Needs pitch deck, 2-3 minute product demo video, Azure architecture diagram, written responses; live interview with demo and Q&A. — [Imagine Cup FAQ](https://imaginecup-stg.microsoft.com/en-us/support/faq)
- GeekWire reports the 2026 rules shifted toward business customers/startups and dropped diversity/inclusion/accessibility from the criteria (search summary; page returned 403 to me). — [GeekWire](https://geekwire.com/2026/microsoft-opens-imagine-cup-to-startups-and-the-new-rules-show-how-its-ai-strategy-has-changed/)

**Other India-oriented published weights (THIRD-PARTY; treat as indicative only)**
- Unstop hiring-hackathon framework (Unstop's own suggestion, not a specific event): Technical Execution 25, Problem Understanding 15, Feasibility 15, Communication 15, Solution Quality 10, Innovation 10, Business/User Impact 10. Top technical band requires "works end to end"; UI mockup or slides score low. — [Unstop blog](https://unstop.com/blog/hackathon-judging-criteria-for-hiring)
- Reskilll blog claims "most Indian hackathons" use Innovation 20-25, Technical 20-25, Impact 20-25, Demo/Presentation 15-20, Completeness 10-15; the blog cites no data. — [Reskilll](https://blogs.reskilll.com/what-hackathon-judges-look-for-complete-judging-criteria-breakdown-2026/)
- Flipkart GRiD: only third-party prep sites describe rounds and criteria (profile-based shortlisting, coding/quiz rounds, final on a business problem judged on solution quality and technical depth); no Flipkart rubric found. — [PrepInsta / Velonx guides via search](https://velonx.in/blog/flipkart-grid-80-complete-guide)

### Inferences
- Across official rubrics, "problem clarity + evidence + working demo + scalability" collectively account for most points; novelty is explicit but not dominant (SIH lists it as one of ~9 factors; Google gives 0 explicit points for novelty in the 2024 rubric).
- The Google 2024 rubric converts directly into a checklist for a screening deck: 3 real-user feedback points, 3 iterations, a metrics/cause-effect slide, an architecture slide with one real engineering challenge, a scalability slide.
- Where weights exist they favour technical execution/completeness (25-40%), so a deck-only entry is at a structural disadvantage if a prototype link is requested.
- Amazon HackOn / Hack2skill / Devfolio-run finals: no official rubric found; do not assume a weight.

### Gaps
- No official weights for SIH, Imagine Cup (any year), Flipkart GRiD, Amazon HackOn, Hack2skill events, or Devfolio events. 2025/2026 Google rubric from Google itself (the FAQ page fetch returned only a community landing page; the 2024 guide is a SlideShare mirror, and a 2025 SlideShare deck reportedly lists Technical Merit 40% / UX 10%, so the rubric varies by year/region).
- HackIndia 2025 national finals handbook (one search summary says Technical Implementation 34%): page returned 404, so unverified.

## 3. Which claims judges distrust, and what evidence makes a claim credible

### Takeaway
Judges distrust unsupported impact numbers, "AI did it" output that is not shown acting, hard-coded demo data, and claims that a weekend prototype scales. Credibility comes from (a) real users outside the team with named feedback and what changed, (b) metrics or an explicit cause-and-effect chain, (c) running software/public repo, (d) honest limitations. Notably, even Google's 2024 winners published no user counts, which suggests the bar is "show your method for evidence", not "have a pilot".

### Cited Findings
- Official Google rubric rewards "three feedback points from real users outside the team", the three improvements made, and results via "metrics, goals, and outcomes, or cause and effect" when numbers are unavailable. — [2024 Participant Guide](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/2024-solution-challenge-participant-guidepptx/265566208)
- A judge's account (Outlook India "hub4business" piece; possibly sponsored content): "Either it runs in front of the judges or it doesn't"; he distrusts fluent agent output presented as proof of action, and distrusts treating a weekend prototype as proof it will hold up at scale; prefers agents that show what they touched, what changed, and how to verify or undo it. Judged Codefest, TISB Hacks 3.0 and Cal Hacks 12.0. — [Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/hub4business/does-it-actually-work-rahul-taraks-decade-at-the-judging-table)
- A judge reviewing many AI/dev projects (dev.to): the gap between "interesting" and "strong" was "execution clarity"; stronger entries were honest about how AI assisted (boilerplate, debugging, docs, tests) rather than claiming AI built everything; judges trusted entries with README, architecture diagram, demo video, screenshots, setup steps and a known-limitations list; security, error handling, privacy, reliability, deployment readiness stood out. — [dev.to judge post](https://dev.to/amising6/what-i-learned-after-reviewing-many-ai-and-developer-projects-as-a-hackathon-judge-2g06)
- DoraHacks guide: vague grandiose language ("revolutionary", "paradigm-shifting") is a common mistake; "Substance beats buzzwords"; judges can only evaluate what they can see (private repos and broken links lose points). — [DoraHacks on dev.to](https://dev.to/dorahacks/what-does-a-good-hackathon-submission-look-like-5398)
- Unstop framework: prototypes with visible gaps can still score mid-band if the team explains the shortcuts honestly; the top band needs the architecture explained "without hand-waving". — [Unstop blog](https://unstop.com/blog/hackathon-judging-criteria-for-hiring)
- Reskilll (uncited, THIRD-PARTY) lists low-impact signals: vague societal-benefit claims, no user research, solutions searching for a problem; illustrative good claims are specific ("ASHA workers in rural Bihar", "saves 2 hours/day"). — [Reskilll](https://blogs.reskilll.com/what-hackathon-judges-look-for-complete-judging-criteria-breakdown-2026/)
- SIH requires ideas to be new and not previously presented at any event; sponsors can decline to award a winner. — [SIH 2024 Guidelines](https://sih.gov.in/letters/Guidelines-College-SPOC.pdf)

### Inferences
- Credible-evidence ladder (my synthesis, ordered strongest to weakest): live system with a reproducible test run > recorded/logged results on a public or synthetic-but-documented dataset with a stated method > named third-party feedback (pilot letter, faculty/placement-cell quote) > cited public statistics > unsourced percentages. Per-claim labelling ("measured / simulated / cited / projected") is a low-cost trust move; no source says this explicitly, it follows from the "cause and effect" and "honest limitations" findings.
- For a placement/EdTech/civic-trust product, safety/ethics claims (fairness, consent, explainability) will be believed only if shown: an audit trail, a failing-case handled, a bias check result.

### Gaps
- No published judge survey on which claim types are penalised in Indian student hackathons specifically; the above is mostly judge anecdotes and organiser guidance.
- Could not find sources quantifying the value of a pilot/LOI letter at the screening stage.

## 4. Demo techniques that have worked (live data, adversarial test, "try to break it", before/after)

### Takeaway
Sources agree on "live, working, real data, with a recorded backup". Before/after metrics are explicitly recommended. I found NO published example of a winner using an organised "try to break it" challenge at a hackathon; the closest is judges' stated preference for visible verify/undo and edge-case handling. Treat "adversarial live test" as a hypothesis, not a proven pattern.

### Cited Findings
- SIH winner first-person account (Reskilll, unverifiable): judges said the live demo with real data made them trust it, and praised edge-case testing and error handling; they did not mention code quality, ML accuracy or tech stack. The team used a strict 3-minute script: problem plus one statistic (10s), live solution with real data (90s), impact claim (20s), architecture (20s), scale claim (10s); replaced a struggling ML model with rules plus an LLM API; added realistic Indian test data, mobile responsiveness, and a recorded backup video. — [Reskilll](https://blogs.reskilll.com/how-i-won-smart-india-hackathon-lessons-36-hours-changed-everything/)
- Judge on "finish-up" challenges: best projects told a before-and-after story rather than showing flashy features; a focused project with a working demo can beat an ambitious one lacking proof. — [dev.to judge post](https://dev.to/amising6/what-i-learned-after-reviewing-many-ai-and-developer-projects-as-a-hackathon-judge-2g06)
- Judge Gleb Bahmutov: do not require judges to log in with personal accounts that request broad permissions (read-only or a demo account instead); automate deployment early; add crash reporting and have friends find edge cases early. — [glebbahmutov.com](https://glebbahmutov.com/blog/winning-hackathons/)
- Judge (Outlook India): make actions, changes and undo paths visible; get it running before the judging moment; publish the code. — [Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/hub4business/does-it-actually-work-rahul-taraks-decade-at-the-judging-table)
- Momen (dev.to, vendor blog): most failures come from over-investing in the frontend and under-investing in whether data saves, logic stays consistent and the demo avoids erroring halfway. — [Momen on dev.to](https://dev.to/momen_hq/hackathon-survival-guide-what-actually-matters-3hme)
- QUASAR-specific: the format has judges propose implementation changes at the start of the 24-hour build — a built-in "show you can respond to feedback" test. — [QUASAR 2.0 Devfolio](https://quasar-2.devfolio.co/)
- Google 2024 rubric explicitly scores a demo video showing a working application, not a mockup, within 2 minutes. — [2024 Participant Guide](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/2024-solution-challenge-participant-guidepptx/265566208)

### Inferences
- A "try to break it" station (judge types an adversarial input and the system either handles it or fails safely with a visible reason) fits the judge preferences above (verify/undo, edge cases, "does it actually do it") and is rare enough to be memorable. It is an inference, not a documented winning technique.
- A before/after panel using the team's own reproducible numbers (e.g., baseline vs. system on the same inputs) is the form most consistent with the "cause and effect" and "before and after" guidance. Label whether numbers are measured on real data, synthetic data, or simulated.
- Prerecorded backup of the live demo is advised by only one first-person source; low cost, so worth doing (inference).

### Gaps
- No video or scoring-sheet evidence of adversarial/"try to break it" demos winning an Indian hackathon.
- No data on how often live demos fail in Indian finals or whether judges penalise recorded backups.

## 5. Patterns of winning submissions (live vs mock-up, numbers, real data, hero idea vs breadth, explainability, safety, scale/cost, India fit)

### Takeaway
Winning patterns in the sources: working > mockup; focused on one problem with specific named users > broad platform; honest about AI role and limits; evidence of user contact; documented architecture with scalability story; India-specific realism (data, language, devices). No source gives a controlled comparison; this is practitioner consensus, not statistics.

### Cited Findings
- Working vs mockup: Google rubric ("actual working application (not a mockup)"); Unstop framework (mockup scores low); Outlook India judge ("slide-deck-led pitches that can't be demonstrated" lose); Phase 1 of the 2026 India Solution Challenge requires a live MVP link and public repo. — sources linked in sections 2-3.
- Hero idea vs breadth: dev.to judge says a focused project with a working demo and clear use case can beat an ambitious one that lacks proof; Reskilll warns about "jumping to tech before understanding the problem, overengineering" (Where U Elevate) and about solutions searching for a problem. — [dev.to judge](https://dev.to/amising6/what-i-learned-after-reviewing-many-ai-and-developer-projects-as-a-hackathon-judge-2g06); [Where U Elevate](https://whereuelevate.com/blogs/smart-india-hackathon-2026)
- Problem fit: SIH winner account says judges valued that the solution "actually addressed the problem statement" rather than something adjacent; the team chose a less crowded problem statement for better odds (with only 500 ideas capped per PS and 4-5 finalists, crowded PS are statistically tough). — [Reskilll](https://blogs.reskilll.com/how-i-won-smart-india-hackathon-lessons-36-hours-changed-everything/); [SIH 2024 Guidelines](https://sih.gov.in/letters/Guidelines-College-SPOC.pdf)
- Specificity of user: "ASHA workers in rural Bihar" style specificity and Indian test data are the recommended form of India fit (THIRD-PARTY). — [Reskilll](https://blogs.reskilll.com/what-hackathon-judges-look-for-complete-judging-criteria-breakdown-2026/)
- Scalability and cost: scored explicitly in Google's rubric (architecture, scalability, next steps) and SIH (sustainability, scale of impact, practicability); Imagine Cup adds investability and customer validation at later stages. — sources linked in section 2.
- Explainability/safety: judge praise for visible "what changed, how to verify or undo", plus known-limitations lists, privacy and security hygiene. — [Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/hub4business/does-it-actually-work-rahul-taraks-decade-at-the-judging-table); [dev.to judge](https://dev.to/amising6/what-i-learned-after-reviewing-many-ai-and-developer-projects-as-a-hackathon-judge-2g06)
- Winners' profiles are modest on evidence: Google's 2024 India winner (Spoon Share) and People's Choice (Saheli) are both mobile apps with no published usage numbers, so a polished, working product with a clear social problem and Google-stack fit can win without a pilot. — [Google blog](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/meet-the-students-that-are-changing-the-world-through-technology/)
- Team structure: small teams can match larger ones (Bahmutov); SIH winner account assigned dedicated backend, frontend, AI/ML and docs/testing/demo roles. — [glebbahmutov.com](https://glebbahmutov.com/blog/winning-hackathons/); [Reskilll](https://blogs.reskilll.com/how-i-won-smart-india-hackathon-lessons-36-hours-changed-everything/)

### Inferences
- The differentiator among ~500 screened decks is probably not novelty alone but verifiable depth: a hero capability whose correctness the judges can check in under a minute (inference; SIH explicitly lists feasibility, practicability and complexity beside novelty).
- For an EdTech/placement/civic-trust idea, explicit fairness, consent, privacy and explainability evidence is under-supplied in the sources' winner examples, so it can be a differentiator if shown concretely rather than listed as a slide bullet (inference).
- A 24-hour on-site format with judge-proposed changes (QUASAR pattern) favours teams with a pre-built, working core who spend the 24 hours on judge feedback, not on building from scratch (inference).

### Gaps
- No access to winning decks themselves (SIH idea PDFs and QUASAR decks are not public); Devpost galleries and Devfolio project pages for Indian events were not examined in this pass.
- No data on pilot letters/LOIs correlating with wins.

## 6. Common failure patterns (generic AI chatbot wrappers, feature lists, unverifiable claims, copied ideas)

### Takeaway
Sources converge on: chat-UI-with-polish agents, slides without code, hard-coded data, vague impact, missing required fields, broken links, feature-list pitches, and incomplete documentation. Plagiarism is formally excluded in SIH ("must be new").

### Cited Findings
- Low-score patterns (Reskilll, uncited): "another chatbot", thin API wrappers, direct copies; slides/mockups without working code; hard-coded data that works only with preloaded inputs; crashes in demo; vague societal-benefit claims; no user research. — [Reskilll](https://blogs.reskilll.com/what-hackathon-judges-look-for-complete-judging-criteria-breakdown-2026/)
- Judge: "agent demos that are essentially a chat interface with a polished front end" lose; "hard problems nobody has" and good ideas whose code does not run yet lose. — [Outlook India](https://www.outlookindia.com/hub4business/does-it-actually-work-rahul-taraks-decade-at-the-judging-table)
- Pitch reduced to a feature list is a "common pitfall" per RevenueCat's hackathon pitch guide (author opinion). — [RevenueCat](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/engineering/how-to-win-shipaton-part-4-pitching-your-app)
- Submission hygiene: missing required fields, exceeded word limits, wrong format can disqualify before judging; private repos, broken demo links, broken access are common losses. — [DoraHacks on dev.to](https://dev.to/dorahacks/what-does-a-good-hackathon-submission-look-like-5398)
- Incomplete documentation can eliminate a strong idea at SIH national shortlisting (third-party). — [Where U Elevate](https://whereuelevate.com/blogs/smart-india-hackathon-2026)
- Over-claiming AI authorship hurts credibility; attractive UI with thin depth is a weakness the judge describes. — [dev.to judge](https://dev.to/amising6/what-i-learned-after-reviewing-many-ai-and-developer-projects-as-a-hackathon-judge-2g06)
- Copied ideas: SIH rules bar ideas that have appeared in any previous event/program. — [SIH 2024 Guidelines](https://sih.gov.in/letters/Guidelines-College-SPOC.pdf)
- Tactical failure: demos requiring broad social-login permissions get skipped. — [glebbahmutov.com](https://glebbahmutov.com/blog/winning-hackathons/)

### Inferences
- With 4-5 finalists per SIH problem statement out of up to 500 ideas (~1%), and many students submitting the same classes of ideas (chatbot, dashboard, resume scorer), "similar to the other 499" is itself the main risk at screening (inference from the funnel numbers; no source states the share of near-duplicate ideas).
- A submission whose main novelty is an LLM call will be read as a wrapper unless the deck shows what is deterministic, measurable, or auditable outside the LLM (inference).

### Gaps
- No source quantifies how common wrappers or copied ideas are in screening rounds.

## 7. Making a screening-round deck and demo video memorable in the first 30 seconds

### Takeaway
Guides agree: open with the target user and a concrete frustration (not "AI"), show the working product early, keep videos short (60-90 seconds per one judge; 2-3 minutes where mandated), and make the first slide/line a statement of what the system does. Judges see dozens to hundreds of entries, so memorability = one specific, checkable thing. These are practitioner heuristics, not tested results.

### Cited Findings
- Judges review "dozens or even hundreds of submissions"; open with what makes the project distinctive so that a judge reading only the opening understands why it deserves attention; open with a concrete statement of what the system does rather than a claim about AI (ForestGuard example: "detects, verifies, reports"); give the judge one specific thing to remember. — [DoraHacks on dev.to](https://dev.to/dorahacks/what-does-a-good-hackathon-submission-look-like-5398)
- First 15-20 seconds: who the app is for and their frustration, one or two instantly recognisable moments; show the app running on the target device after the need is set up; hook and proof within 60-90 seconds; 3 minutes max; use a quick before/after with numbers; end with a one-line transformation and call to action. — [RevenueCat Shipaton guide](https://www.revenuecat.com/blog/engineering/how-to-win-shipaton-part-4-pitching-your-app)
- Judge Bahmutov: anything over 60-90 seconds is too long; avoid long intros. — [glebbahmutov.com](https://glebbahmutov.com/blog/winning-hackathons/)
- AI Tinkerers: production quality does not affect judging score; judges look for a working demo and an innovative solution (search-result summary). — [AI Tinkerers video guide](https://aitinkerers.org/docs/public/video_production_guide.md?markdown=1)

### Inferences (concrete tactics derived from the above; not themselves sourced)
- Slide 1 / second 0-10: one named user, one number-backed frustration, and a one-line "what it does" claim that is falsifiable. Second 10-30: cut straight to the product producing a result on real/realistic data with the claim shown on screen.
- Use a visible "proof tag" on each claim (measured / simulated / cited) so skim-reading judges can see it is verifiable.
- Put a runnable link, public repo, and a "try this input" prompt on the first slide, so the 500-entry reviewer can verify in 30 seconds.
- Show one failure handled safely (adversarial/edge input with a visible reason) in the first minute; rare in decks, consistent with judge distrust of "fluent output as proof".
- Provide a read-only demo login or no login at all; keep the deck inside any page limit; match the video beats to the write-up (RevenueCat/DoraHacks).
